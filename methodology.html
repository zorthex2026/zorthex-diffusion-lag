00 · The Lineage

The Missing Variable

Rogers, Bass, and Moore built the model. Zorthex measures what they could not.

This is the perspective of an independent researcher with a background in the humanities. No institutional affiliation, no laboratory. Just a question that wouldn't let go — and the public data to begin answering it.

There are ideas that define a field so completely that we forget they were once radical. Everett Rogers published Diffusion of Innovations in 1962. Frank Bass added the mathematical architecture in 1969. Geoffrey Moore identified the structural gap that kills most innovations in 1991. Together, they gave us the language and the tools to understand one of the most fundamental questions in social and economic life: how do new things spread — and why do so many of them fail?

I want to start with gratitude. Before proposing anything new, it is worth understanding — precisely — what these three thinkers actually built. Because the more carefully you read them, the more you realise that what looks like a limitation is often a deliberate choice, made with the tools available at the time.

How Rogers understood time

Rogers did not treat time as background. He made it the third of four explicit elements in his definition of diffusion — a definition so precise it has survived sixty years without revision:

"Diffusion is the process by which an innovation is communicated through certain channels, over time, among the members of a social system."

Time enters his framework in three distinct ways: through the innovation-decision process (how long an individual takes to move from awareness to adoption), through innovativeness (how early or late a person adopts relative to others), and through the rate of adoption (how fast an innovation spreads through a system).

This is a sophisticated treatment of time. But it is time as experienced by individuals inside a social process — relative, qualitative, endogenous. Rogers measures how long people take to decide. He does not — and with the tools of 1962, could not — measure how long a phenomenon takes to become visible to the world outside.

How Bass formalised time

Bass took Rogers' sociology and gave it a differential equation. In his 1969 paper, time becomes the independent variable: dN/dt — the rate of change of cumulative adopters with respect to time. For the first time, diffusion had a mathematical clock.

His formula is elegant precisely because of what it assumes: that diffusion is continuous, driven by two stable internal forces — innovators (p) and imitators (q) — and that the system is closed. The wave builds from within. Word of mouth does the work. The curve is smooth.

Bass was not naive about this. He knew his model worked best for consumer durables — televisions, washing machines, the products he studied. He was building a tool for a specific problem, and he built it brilliantly.

Moore's Chasm: the map of why innovations die

Geoffrey Moore gave us the most honest map of why innovations fail. Not because they are bad. Not because the market doesn't need them. But because there is a structural gap — the Chasm — between the Early Adopters who buy on vision and the Early Majority who buy on proof.

The pragmatists don't care about potential. They care about references. They want to see someone like them, in a company like theirs, who has already solved the problem and lived to tell the story. Without that reference, the wave breaks before it reaches the shore.

Moore's insight was surgical: the visionaries are not credible references for the pragmatists. They speak different languages, they measure success differently, they buy for different reasons. And so, after the initial excitement of the early market, sales stall. The chasm opens. Most innovations never cross it.

What they could not see — and what we can now measure

Here is the thing none of them could observe: they had no way to distinguish between a phenomenon that exists and a phenomenon that is visible.

In 1962, in 1969, in 1991, there was no instrument that could tell you — in real time, across millions of people — when a technology had crossed from specialist awareness into public attention. You could survey. You could interview. You could study sales data after the fact. But you could not watch the moment of transition happening.

Today we can.

Search volumes, encyclopaedic pageviews, citation frequencies in specialist versus generalist sources — these are instruments Rogers, Bass, and Moore never had. And what they reveal, across decades of documented cases, is a pattern that their models do not capture.

For years — sometimes decades — a phenomenon lives entirely within a specialist layer. The technical community knows it. Experts write about it. The knowledge passes from hand to hand, carefully, like a candle sheltered from the wind. Bass's imitators are there, but they imitate only within the specialist network. Rogers' Early Adopters are active — but their activity is invisible to the general public. Moore's chasm sits open, waiting.

Then something arrives. Not the critical mass of imitators Bass modelled. Not the word of mouth Rogers described. Not the peer reference Moore prescribed. An external event — a regulation, an institutional mandate, a policy trigger — arrives and changes the regime entirely.

The candle becomes the Olympic torch. It does not grow gradually brighter. It ignites.

The distinction that matters: endogenous vs exogenous time

This is not a refutation of Rogers, Bass, or Moore. It is a distinction they could not make with the data available to them.

All three models assume that diffusion is driven by forces endogenous to the system — communication between adopters, imitation of innovators, peer references among pragmatists. Time, in all three frameworks, is shaped by internal social dynamics.

What the data now shows is that for entire categories of phenomena — cybersecurity, cryptography, AI governance, financial infrastructure, regulated markets — time is shaped by forces exogenous to the system. A regulation does not spread through word of mouth. It arrives. And when it arrives, it does not accelerate an existing curve. It switches the regime.

This distinction matters practically. If you are an investor, a strategist, or a policy maker trying to understand when a phenomenon will break through to the mainstream, the Bass model tells you to watch the imitator network. Moore tells you to watch the reference base. In policy-driven domains, both watches are the wrong ones. The signal is not inside the network. It is outside it — in the regulatory calendar, in the institutional pipeline, in the structural gap between when a norm exists and when it becomes operationally binding.

That gap is now measurable. Not as a prediction — Zorthex does not forecast. But as an empirical observation: how long, across documented cases, has that gap historically been? What regime is a given phenomenon in? How far along the specialist attention curve has it travelled before the trigger arrives?

Why this matters now more than ever

Rogers wrote before the internet. Bass wrote before digital markets. Moore wrote before platforms and network effects reshaped entire industries overnight. All three built their models in a world where noise was limited by the friction of communication.

Today the opposite problem exists. Communication is instantaneous and global. Noise is everywhere. Every technology is announced, hyped, declared revolutionary, and buried — sometimes within the same news cycle.

In this environment, the ability to distinguish signal from noise is not just analytically useful. It is the difference between acting on evidence and acting on narrative.

Time — precisely measured, empirically grounded, stripped of hype — becomes the most valuable variable of all. Not time as Rogers described it, the duration of individual decision processes. Not time as Bass modelled it, the continuous accumulation of imitators. Not time as Moore implied it, the undefined wait before a reference base forms. But time as the distance between operational reality and public visibility. Time as the measure of how long a phenomenon can exist before the world decides to look at it.

That distance — that lag — is what Zorthex measures. It is built on the foundation Rogers, Bass, and Moore laid. It could not exist without them.

01 · Core Framework

The Diffusion Lag

Zorthex measures one thing: how long a phenomenon exists before the public discovers it. This gap — between operational reality and public attention — is the diffusion lag (L), measured in months.

L = t_peak − t_start
t_start = first documented emergence (institutional, not conceptual) · t_peak = first month Google Trends ≥ 25/100

t_start — When It Began

The moment a phenomenon becomes operationally real — not when it was theorised, not when it was first mentioned, but when it became infrastructure that could be used. For stablecoins: October 2014 (Tether launched, not when the idea was proposed). For SMR: May 2005 (IAEA published the framework, not when nuclear energy started). Every t_start is documented with a primary source and a confidence level.

t_peak — When the Public Noticed

The first month Google Trends reaches ≥25/100 for the phenomenon's query, worldwide. This is not peak attention — it's the threshold where search interest becomes structurally measurable. t_peak is always after t_start. The gap between them is L.

Structural Threshold

12 consecutive months ≥ 25/100 on Google Trends = STRUCTURAL. The term has entered the public vocabulary. The threshold is conventional — like BBB in credit ratings or p<0.05 in statistics. It is not "correct." It is stable, declared, and replicable. Anyone can download the same CSV and verify.

Why 25? Google Trends normalises to 0–100 relative to the query's own peak. A score of 25 means the search volume is 25% of its all-time peak. Below 25, the signal is intermittent — monthly noise. Above 25 for 12 consecutive months, the signal is sustained. The choice is conventional. The threshold is declared so anyone can recalculate with a different value.
02 · Three-Source Verification

Three Clocks. Same Direction — or Not.

Every case is positioned using three independent attention signals. They measure different things at different levels of rigour. The strength is not that all three are quantitative — it's that they are independent.

Google Trends
Operational attention. Who searches to act. CSV locked, timestamped, archived. L is computed here. Primary metric — quantitative, reproducible.
Wikipedia
Informational attention. Who reads to understand. Wikimedia API, daily granularity. Independent corrective to Google Trends normalisation.
Reddit
Community attention. How it's discussed. Declared qualitative proxy (assente / nascente / matura). Not a metric — a directional signal.

Why Not Social Media?

Zorthex measures intentional attention — a person decides to search or read. Social media measures passive exposure — an algorithm decides what you see. The first is demand. The second is supply.

INTENTIONAL (ZORTHEX)
  • Reproducible. CSV locked, anyone can verify.
  • Resistant to manipulation. No paid promotion on GT.
  • Measures demand. What people want to know.
  • Open. Google Trends and Wikipedia are free, timestamped, archivable.
PASSIVE (NOT USED)
  • Non-reproducible. Feeds are personalised.
  • Manipulable. Bots, paid amplification, viral mechanics.
  • Measures supply. What algorithms show you.
  • Proprietary. API-gated, paywalled, retroactively changeable.

The difference is between a thermometer and a heater. Social media is a heater. Zorthex reads the thermometer.

03 · Classification System

What Happened — Permanent

Classification answers one question: did this phenomenon enter the public vocabulary? Once earned, permanent — like a credit rating at issuance. The data that produced the classification is locked and archived.

STRUCTURAL
12+ consecutive months ≥ 25/100

The term entered the public vocabulary. Permanent classification. The 12-month rule is the rule. No exceptions, no margin adjustment.

OBSERVATION
Above threshold, <12 months

The window is open. Consolidation not yet confirmed. May promote to STRUCTURAL or retreat to Spike & Retreat.

SPIKE & RETREAT
Reached threshold, did not sustain 12 months

Breakout occurred, consolidation did not. Attention rose above 25 but fell back before completing 12 consecutive months. The phenomenon was noticed but not absorbed. New category, August 2026 revision. 10 cases identified.

BUBBLE
Sharp spike, rapid collapse

Short, intense attention burst that collapsed. Never sustained. The term was noise, not signal. Examples: Metaverse (9 max), NFT (8 max), ICO Boom (3 max).

PRE / NON-STAT
Peak precedes data or confounded

The phenomenon's peak predates Google Trends data (pre-2004) or the attention signal is confounded by an external shock (e.g. COVID). Retained for completeness, excluded from L averaging.

04 · Operational Status — New, August 2026

What's Happening Now — Updated Every 90 Days

The same STRUCTURAL classification can mean very different things. Streaming (218 months above threshold) and SMR (12 months exact, then retreated) both carry STRUCTURAL. The classification is correct for both. The operational reality is different.

Operational status is the second axis — a weather reading on top of the climate classification. It is applied only to STRUCTURAL cases and updated at every 90-day revision. It does not modify the classification. It describes the current state of attention.

DOMINANT
Above threshold for 40+ consecutive months

Permanent vocabulary. Will not return below threshold. Examples: Streaming (218mo), iPhone (208mo), TikTok (78mo), ESG (69mo).

ACTIVE
Above threshold right now

Consolidated and alive. The term is current. Examples: Stablecoins (~31), RWA Tokenization (40), mRNA Cancer Vaccines (100).

RECEDING
STRUCTURAL but currently below threshold

The term consolidated once — attention is now fading. May return (cyclical) or continue declining (terminal). Examples: SMR (23, 12mo exact then retreated), Basel III (24, mature term in decline).

CYCLICAL
Above and below threshold in waves

Tied to market cycles or recurring events. Not a decline — a rhythm. Examples: Bitcoin (21, follows market cycles), Cryptocurrency (20).

DEAD
The term has been replaced or abandoned

Near-zero score, no prospect of return. The classification is permanent — the word is not. Example: Facebook (5, replaced by "Meta").

Example reading: "RWA Tokenization · STRUCTURAL · Active · 40/100" → the technology entered the vocabulary (STRUCTURAL), is still above threshold (Active), with current attention at 40. Contrast: "SMR · STRUCTURAL · Receding · 23/100" → same classification, different story. Both are correct. The operational status tells you which decline is a victory and which is a retreat.
